02 — Problem

Problem Statement

Over 1.3 billion people are expected to use digital health tools in 2025. 44% of US adults already own a wearable device — yet their health data remains scattered across a dozen apps, none of which talk to each other.

Existing platforms solve only one piece of the puzzle: wearables track isolated metrics, telehealth apps offer reactive consultations, wellness apps surface generic advice. Nobody synthesizes it all into a single, actionable picture — and nobody proactively warns you before something goes wrong.

Market reality
44% of US adults own a wearable health device, up from just 21% in 2019 — yet most still can't answer a simple question: "How healthy am I, really?"
01
Fragmented health data
People use multiple wearables and apps (Apple Watch, Garmin, Fitbit, Whoop) but their data lives in silos — no unified view exists.
02
Devices collect heart rate, sleep, steps, oxygen — but none translate raw numbers into personalized guidance or health actions.
No actionable insights
03
Reactive, not preventive
People lack early warning systems for stress, metabolic risks, or heart conditions. Healthcare is consulted only after symptoms appear.

04 — Design Challenges

Key Design Problems I Solved

Each feature came with a concrete UX challenge. Here's what I was actually designing for — not just what it looks like, but why it works.

01
Health Score as a single source of truth
Users receive data from 200+ sources. The challenge: collapse heart rate, steps, sleep, calories, oxygen into one number (0–100) that feels trustworthy and personally meaningful — without oversimplifying. Designed a gauge component with contextual color, history sparkline, and drill-down sub-scores.
02
Zero-friction wearable onboarding
Each wearable company exposes data differently (Webhooks, REST, OAuth). The user sees none of this — just a single "Connect" flow. Designed permission screens that explain value before asking for access, with Apple Health / Google Fit integration completing in under 2 taps.
03
Medical AI chat that doesn't feel clinical
MedPal provides non-clinical health guidance and must never be mistaken for medical advice. Designed the chat UX with clear "Health Expert" persona framing, warm tone, and explicit escalation paths to licensed professionals — while keeping the experience conversational and reassuring.
04
Expert profiles — trust through design
AI experts are represented as named specialists (Dr. Kevin O'Neill, Charlie Williams etc.) with real credentials, patient counts, and specializations. Designed profile cards that establish authority at a glance — credential badges, experience counters, specialty color-coding — so users feel confident sharing sensitive data.
05
Voice UX for health — a new interaction paradigm
Designing a voice-first health agent required rethinking the visual layer entirely: what does the UI show when the user is speaking? Designed a minimal listening state, animated waveform, and contextual response cards that surface data visually while voice narrates the insight — complementary modalities, not competing ones.
